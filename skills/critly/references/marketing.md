# Marketing

## Message First

> Say what only you do, then lay out the page.

- Section: Marketing
- URL: https://critly.vercel.app/message-first
- Published: 2026-10-14
- Source: https://github.com/kausthubh-coder/critly/blob/main/content/marketing/message-first.mdx

In our evals, AI-built landing pages kept opening the same way: a big
headline that could sit on any product's page, something like "Work smarter,
together", a grey line under it, two buttons. Then eleven tidy sections.
The spacing is right, the contrast passes, and you still can't tell what the
product does. There was
nothing to lay out, because no one decided what the page was saying.

**Before you choose a single section, write down who the page is for, what
they use today, and what only this product does, because no layout can rescue
a message any competitor could say.**

> **Interactive demo: Message First.** Open https://critly.vercel.app/message-first to try it.

Pick a product and step through its three drafts. The first could sell
anything. The last names a thing you can picture, a claim that could turn out
false, and a difference the competitor can't copy by changing the logo. Swap
in the competitor's name to test it.

### Start from what they'd use instead

April Dunford's positioning process runs in a fixed order: the alternatives
customers would use if you didn't exist, then what you have that those
alternatives don't, the value that follows, who cares most about it, and last,
the category. She [explains why the order matters](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning):
starting anywhere else gave her "positioning that sounded good in the office,
but it didn't work with customers because it wasn't differentiated."

The alternative is rarely another startup. It is a spreadsheet, a clipboard
by the dock, a phone call, or doing nothing. Naming it tells you what the page
has to beat, and it gives you the reader's words, because that's the thing
they already know.

So before any section exists, write four lines:

- **For:** the people who care most, narrowly enough that someone could say
  "that's not me".
- **Instead of:** what they do today.
- **Only:** what this product does that the alternative and the nearest
  competitor can't.
- **So:** what changes for them, in a thing they could see happen.

The third line is the "only" line from the [direction note](https://critly.vercel.app/direction-first),
rewritten for a buyer. The headline usually comes straight out of the third
and fourth.

### Three tests for every line

Harry Dry asks three questions of any line of copy, as
[Demand Curve reports](https://www.demandcurve.com/newsletters/growth-newsletter-202):
can you visualize it, can you falsify it, and can nobody else say it? "If you
have three no's, you've probably written a lot of rubbish."

**Visualize** means a reader can picture something: a boat, a berth, a loaf.
"Streamline operations" has no picture. **Falsify** means the claim could be
checked and found untrue. "Better insights" can't be wrong, so it says
nothing. **Nobody else** is the competitor test: put the nearest competitor's
name in front of the line. If it still reads true, it's their headline too.

Julian Shapiro's [version of the same test](https://www.julian.com/guide/growth/landing-pages)
is for the header alone: if a visitor reads only that text, will they know
exactly what you sell? His examples of headers that fail are "Improve your
workflow!" and "Supercharge your collaboration!".

### The message picks the sections

Once the four lines exist, they answer most layout questions. The "only"
line becomes the headline and decides what the first screen
[has to show](https://critly.vercel.app/show-the-product). The "instead of" line becomes the comparison
the page needs. Every claim needs [proof](https://critly.vercel.app/specific-proof) next to it, and
every doubt a buyer could have becomes a section, in the order they'd
[ask it](https://critly.vercel.app/sections-follow-questions).

Without them, sections get chosen from a template, and each one gets filled
with whatever sounds plausible. That's how a page ends up with eleven equal
sections of small grey text: none of them had a job.

How sure is this? Dunford, Dry and Shapiro are practitioners writing from
client work and tests they ran, not from controlled studies. The time pressure
behind it is measured: in Nielsen Norman Group's analysis of page visits,
people are most likely to leave in the
[first 10 seconds](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/),
and a page has that long to say what it offers. A headline that needs the rest
of the page to make sense spends those seconds on nothing.

## The First Screen

> Say what it is, show it, offer one next step.

- Section: Marketing
- URL: https://critly.vercel.app/the-first-screen
- Published: 2026-10-14
- Source: https://github.com/kausthubh-coder/critly/blob/main/content/marketing/the-first-screen.mdx

In our evals, AI-built landing pages opened on a headline that didn't say what
the product was, body text at 14px on a desktop screen, and almost no picture
of the product. The first screen looked finished. Nothing on it said what the
product was, who it was for, or what it looked like.

**On the first screen, say what the product is and who it's for in plain
words, show it, and offer one next step, in a page shape people already
recognize, because that screen decides whether anyone reads the rest.**

> **Interactive demo: First Screen.** Open https://critly.vercel.app/the-first-screen to try it.

Switch between the weak and strong versions at both sizes. Then flash each
one for 50ms and ask yourself what you saw. The weak version leaves you with a
shape and a color. The strong one leaves the product.

### Fifty milliseconds, then ten seconds

People make two quick decisions about a page. In a Google study by
[Tuch and colleagues](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/),
participants rated 119 real sites shown for as little as 50ms, and complexity
registered in as little as 17ms. Sites that were visually simple and looked
typical of their kind were liked best. That's a lab measure of appeal, not of
sales, but it says something useful: a familiar layout (name top left, a
headline, a picture, a button) isn't what makes a page generic. Generic copy
and generic pictures do. Keep the shape people know and make the content
specific.

The second decision is whether to stay. In Nielsen Norman Group's analysis of
page visit times, people are most likely to leave in the
[first 10 seconds](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/),
and a page that wants minutes of attention has to make its value clear in
that time. Ten seconds is a headline, a line under it, and a look at the
picture. If the headline is a slogan, those seconds are gone.

### Most attention stays near the top

People scroll, but attention thins as they go. In
[Nielsen Norman Group's 2018 eye-tracking](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/),
57% of viewing time went to the first screenful and 74% to the first two,
down from 80% above the fold in 2010. So the first screen can't be a teaser
for the real content below. It has to carry what it is, who it's for, the
product, and the next step on its own.

It also has to admit there's more. A hero that fills the window exactly makes
the page look complete. In one of
[their studies](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/),
six of eight users didn't realize they could scroll a page whose full-height
hero ended on a clean line. Let the top of the next section show at both
desktop and phone sizes, so the page invites a scroll without an arrow
asking for one.

### Big enough to lead

A dense tool can use 14px body text, because people read it in glances all
day. A page that persuades gets read once, by someone deciding whether to care.
Body text of 17 to 20px is a good default for it, with a headline big enough
that it's plainly the first thing you read. Pick a larger ratio than an app
would, and let it shrink on phones. Utopia's
[fluid scales](https://utopia.fyi/blog/designing-with-fluid-type-scales/) use
a ratio of about 1.2 on a small screen and 1.33 on a large one, and
`clamp()` moves between them.

```css
:root {
  --text-body: clamp(1.0625rem, 1rem + 0.3vw, 1.25rem);   /* 17px → 20px */
  --text-display: clamp(2.5rem, 1.6rem + 4vw, 4.5rem);    /* 40px → 72px */
}

h1 {
  font: 600 var(--text-display) / 1.05 var(--font-display);
  max-width: 16ch;
  text-wrap: balance;
}
```

The top step has to fit the real headline. If the headline is a full sentence
naming the product and the reader, try it at full size on a phone before you
settle the scale. The [type scale](https://critly.vercel.app/type-scale) sets the steps, and
[line length](https://critly.vercel.app/line-length) keeps the body readable at the larger size.

### One next step

Two equal buttons ask the reader to choose before they know anything. Pick the
one action most people should take, make it the only solid button, and name
it for what happens next. Anything else is a plain link beside it. The
[calls to action](https://critly.vercel.app/calls-to-action) concept covers the wording. The picture
beside it should be the product doing its job, which is
[the next concept](https://critly.vercel.app/show-the-product).

- [Visual complexity and prototypicality in first impressions](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/): Tuch and colleagues at Google (2012). Across 119 sites, simple pages that looked typical of their kind were liked best, and people judged within 50ms.
- [How long do users stay on web pages?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/): Jakob Nielsen on why leaving is most likely in the first 10 seconds, and what a page has to say in that time.
- [Scrolling and attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/): Nielsen Norman Group's 2018 eye-tracking update. 57% of viewing time above the fold, 74% in the first two screens.
- [The illusion of completeness](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/): How a full-screen hero makes a page look finished, so six of eight users never scrolled.

## Show the Product

> Picture the product doing its job.

- Section: Marketing
- URL: https://critly.vercel.app/show-the-product
- Published: 2026-10-14
- Source: https://github.com/kausthubh-coder/critly/blob/main/content/marketing/show-the-product.mdx

Ask a model for a landing page without screenshots and it tends to reach for
what it can draw without knowing the product: a blurred gradient, some glassy
rounded shapes, maybe an illustration of two people at a laptop. In our evals, most pages
had almost no picture of the product at all. The visitor had to take the
headline's word for it.

**Fill the hero and feature images with the product doing its job, its real
screen, its output, its code or the thing itself, because people study
pictures that carry information and skip the ones that only decorate.**

> **Interactive demo: Show The Product.** Open https://critly.vercel.app/show-the-product to try it.

Pick a product, then switch the picture. Shapes and the illustration fit all
three products, and every one of their competitors. Only the product picture
tells you anything you didn't already read in the headline.

### What people look at

Nielsen Norman Group's eye-tracking found two kinds of picture. People
[look closely](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/) at
images that carry information about the task in front of them, and ignore
"big feel-good images that are purely decorative". On one team page, a user
spent 10% more time on the staff portraits than on the bios next to them,
though the bios took up 316% more space.

The same research has a warning. On a shop's TV listings, each product photo
showed a different scene on the screen: a canoe, a football player. People
barely looked at them, because the pictures didn't help them choose. An image
carries information only when it shows what makes this product different from
the next one. A gradient, a glass blob or a stock team scene could sit on any
page in the category, so it says nothing about this one.

These findings come from usability studies, not from conversion tests, and
they're about attention. They fit what practitioners report: Julian Shapiro's
[landing page guide](https://www.julian.com/guide/growth/landing-pages)
tells software companies to "avoid useless abstract imagery" and show the
product in action, since seeing what you're signing up for lowers the risk of
trying it.

### Drawn counts

You don't need a screenshot to show a product. When there isn't one yet, as
when an agent builds a page from a brief, draw it in HTML, CSS or SVG:

- **An app:** its main screen in the middle of a task, with data that looks
  like a real customer's ([real content](https://critly.vercel.app/real-content)), not placeholder bars.
- **A developer tool:** the command and what it prints, or the few lines of
  code it takes.
- **A physical thing:** its outline at true proportions, with the part that
  makes it different called out.

A drawn product stays sharp at every size, follows the page's theme in light
and dark, and weighs a few kilobytes instead of a large hero image
([image loading](https://critly.vercel.app/image-loading)). Draw the product, not shapes about it.

### Pick the moment and the size

A screenshot of an empty dashboard is still a picture of nothing. Show the
moment the product earns its place: the gap flagged before Saturday's service,
the 86% saving printed after the command, the lock folded down to 18 cm. That moment should prove the headline's claim
([message first](https://critly.vercel.app/message-first)).

Make it big enough to read at desktop size. On a phone, crop to the part that
matters rather than shrinking the whole screen until the text is a texture.
Decoration still has a job: a field of the brand color, a texture from the
subject's world, the signature move from the direction. It sets the tone
around the product. It doesn't stand in for it.

### Resources

- [Photos as web content](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/): Jakob Nielsen's eye-tracking findings. People study images that carry information and ignore the ones that only decorate.
- [Decorative images](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/decorative-images/): A short Nielsen Norman Group video on when a decorative image helps the tone and when it gets in the way.
- [Landing page copywriting](https://www.julian.com/guide/growth/landing-pages): Julian Shapiro on showing the product in action in the hero, and why it lowers the risk of signing up.


## Sections Follow Questions

> Order the page by what buyers ask.

- Section: Marketing
- URL: https://critly.vercel.app/sections-follow-questions
- Published: 2026-10-14
- Source: https://github.com/kausthubh-coder/critly/blob/main/content/marketing/sections-follow-questions.mdx

One AI-built landing page from our evals had eleven sections, all of equal
weight and all set in the same small grey 14px text. Scrolling it felt like
reading one section eleven times. Nothing told you which part mattered, so
you skimmed all of it and kept none.

**Order a long page by the questions a buyer asks, in the order they ask them,
give each section one idea, and size it by how much that answer matters,
because a page of equal sections tells the reader nothing is more important
than anything else.**

> **Interactive demo: Sections Follow Questions.** Open https://critly.vercel.app/sections-follow-questions to try it.

The page starts in a common template order. Move sections until no answer
comes before its question, then switch to sized sections and compare the
minimaps.

### Length follows the questions

A landing page can't hear objections, so it has to answer them in advance.
Conversion Rate Experts made that point with Moz: Rand Fishkin needed around
nine minutes to sell the paid product face to face, so they built a page
[six times longer](https://conversion-rate-experts.com/scrolling-tips) than
the original, covering the questions people asked him. It beat the short page
by 52%.

That's one test, for one product with a complex offer, so treat it as a
reason rather than a rule. It doesn't show that long pages win. It shows that
a page that answers the questions people really have can afford to be long.
Eleven sections are fine if each answers a question someone has. Three are
too many if two of them repeat the headline.

### The buyer's order

For most products, a buyer's questions come in roughly this order. It's our
default, built from the sources below, not a measured sequence:

1. What is it, and is it for me?
2. Show me it doing my job.
3. Why not keep doing what I do now?
4. Will it handle my case?
5. Who else uses it, and is it going to last?
6. What does it cost?
7. What if something goes wrong?
8. How do I start?

Your [message](https://critly.vercel.app/message-first) changes the details. The "instead of" line
becomes question three, and the buyer's doubts become questions four and
seven. Julian Shapiro's
[template](https://www.julian.com/guide/growth/landing-pages) puts social
proof and a call to action straight after the hero, then features that answer
objections, then another call to action. He warns that the further a page
strays from a familiar template, the more confused visitors get. Either order
works when each section answers the question the reader has at that point.

Real pages change the order a little and keep its shape. In October 2026,
[Basecamp's homepage](https://basecamp.com/) says what it is, shows a large
screenshot of a real project, walks through the tools one by one, then hands
over to a long letter from its founder before the customer quotes.
[Stripe's payments page](https://stripe.com/payments) puts a working checkout
straight under the headline, then gives one benefit per section and one
interface per product. These are examples of order, not evidence that it works.

Each section gets one idea, and its heading states the answer. "Features" is
a label. "Prices every recipe from your supplier invoices" tells a bakery
owner whether it handles their case.

### Size by weight, and vary the pace

Three equal cards say three things matter equally. Usually one of them is the
reason people buy, and it deserves most of the space and the biggest picture,
with the others as a line each. The same goes for whole sections. The answer
to the first two questions gets the most room. A supporting point gets a
short, tight row.

Vary density as you go. A large product shot, then a compact grid of small
features, then one big quote, then a table people scan, then a short list of
questions. The change from section to section is what tells the eye a new
idea has started, the same way [whitespace](https://critly.vercel.app/whitespace) and
[visual hierarchy](https://critly.vercel.app/visual-hierarchy) do inside one screen. Body text stays
the same comfortable size all the way down (see
[the first screen](https://critly.vercel.app/the-first-screen)). What changes is how much each section
holds and how big its picture is.

### Resources

- [How to make users scroll down your page](https://conversion-rate-experts.com/scrolling-tips): Conversion Rate Experts on the long Moz page that beat a short one by 52%, and why it worked.
- [Landing page copywriting](https://www.julian.com/guide/growth/landing-pages): Julian Shapiro's section order for a landing page, and features written to answer objections.
- [Basecamp](https://basecamp.com/): A long homepage that answers one question per section, with sections of very different size and density.
- [Stripe Payments](https://stripe.com/payments): A product page that shows working UI straight after the headline, then one benefit per section.


## Specific Proof

> Names and numbers, next to the claim.

- Section: Marketing
- URL: https://critly.vercel.app/specific-proof
- Published: 2026-10-14
- Source: https://github.com/kausthubh-coder/critly/blob/main/content/marketing/specific-proof.mdx

"This tool changed how our team works," says Sarah K. Under the quote, a row
of grey logos. Readers have seen that block on so many pages that they skip
it. Nothing in it can be checked, so it adds nothing to the page.

**On a page that persuades, put proof right next to the claim it backs, and make it specific enough to check: a named person with their role and company, or a number with its baseline and timeframe, because praise nobody can check reads as filler.**

> **Interactive demo: Specific Proof.** Open https://critly.vercel.app/specific-proof to try it.

Turn the details on one at a time. A name alone does little, since anyone can
type a name. A number alone raises a question: 18% of what, down from where?
The number starts to mean something once the baseline is there, and the role
and company tell readers whether the result could apply to them.

### Specific means checkable

People expect a company to quote only its happiest customers. In Nielsen
Norman Group's research on [trustworthy design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/),
participants treated a site's own testimonials with "a healthy dose of
skepticism" and trusted reviews on outside sites more. Buyers go and look:
in Wynter's survey of 100 B2B SaaS marketing leaders, [81% check third-party
reviews](https://learn.g2.com/how-b2b-saas-marketing-leaders-buy-software-in-2024)
before they buy software. That is a survey of one kind of buyer, not a test,
but it agrees with what NN/g saw.

So proof on your own page works when it survives that check. A full name, a
role and a company can be looked up. A number with a baseline can be compared
with the reader's own. Linking to where the proof lives, such as a review site
profile, a case study or a public changelog, shows you expect to be checked.
It's Harry Dry's [second test](https://www.demandcurve.com/newsletters/growth-newsletter-202)
for copy: can you prove it false? Vague praise can't be proved false, so it
proves nothing.

Don't count on specific words simply sounding truer. A 2010 lab study found
that concretely worded statements were judged more likely to be true, and a
preregistered replication found the effect [much smaller](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1177/0146167210386238/)
than first reported. Details help because a reader can test them and can tell
whether the result fits their situation. Specific wording on its own doesn't
fool anyone.

### A number needs its baseline

"Saved 40%" leaves out of what, compared with what, over how long, and for
whom. Give the number its baseline (from 31% to 18%), its unit, its timeframe
(in the first quarter) and its source (one fleet of 42 trucks). A modest
number with all four tells the reader more than a large round one with none.
If the figure is one customer's result, say so. If it's an average, say
across how many.

Pick proof from customers who look like the reader. A dispatcher at a
42-truck fleet is better proof for the next 40-truck fleet than a famous logo
that used one feature once.

### Put it next to the claim

A reader doubts a claim at the moment they read it. A quote about speed
belongs beside the speed claim, and a quote about setup belongs beside the
setup steps. A testimonials section at the bottom makes the reader remember
which claim each quote was answering. This is reasoning about how people
read, not a measured result, but it follows from the same idea as
[sections that follow questions](https://critly.vercel.app/sections-follow-questions): each section
answers one question, and its proof answers "does it work for people like
me?"

The same goes for logos. A logo says a company uses the product, not for what
or how much. A logo with one line under it, such as "Ridgeline Freight, 600
loads a week", does the work the bare logo can't.

### Never make it up

A page built before there are customers has no testimonials, and an agent
building one is tempted to invent them. Don't. Invented quotes are the
generic content readers have learned to skip, and in the US the FTC's 2024
rule [bans testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)
from people who don't exist, AI-generated ones included. Use what is real: a
benchmark with its method, the number of teams in the beta, the founder's own
results, the product itself running on the page (see
[show the product](https://critly.vercel.app/show-the-product)). Where the real proof isn't in yet,
leave a clearly marked slot for the owner to fill, as you would for any
[real content](https://critly.vercel.app/real-content) you don't have.

### Resources

- [Trustworthy design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/): Nielsen Norman Group on what makes a site credible. Participants met a company's own testimonials with skepticism and trusted reviews on outside sites more.
- [How B2B SaaS marketing leaders buy software](https://learn.g2.com/how-b2b-saas-marketing-leaders-buy-software-in-2024): G2 on Wynter's survey of 100 B2B SaaS marketing leaders. 81% check third-party reviews when vetting software, and 73% weigh word of mouth most.
- [Harry Dry's three copy tests](https://www.demandcurve.com/newsletters/growth-newsletter-202): Can you picture it, can you prove it false, and could nobody else say it. The second test is the one proof has to pass.
- [Concrete wording and truth, replicated](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1177/0146167210386238/): The FORRT replication atlas entry for Hansen and Wänke (2010). A preregistered replication found the "concrete sounds truer" effect much smaller than first reported.
- [FTC rule on fake reviews and testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials): The 2024 US rule that bans testimonials from people who don't exist, including AI-generated ones, or that misstate a real person's experience.


## Calls to Action

> One action, named for what happens next.

- Section: Marketing
- URL: https://critly.vercel.app/calls-to-action
- Published: 2026-10-14
- Source: https://github.com/kausthubh-coder/critly/blob/main/content/marketing/calls-to-action.mdx

Many heroes end in three buttons the same size and color: "Get started",
"Learn more" and "Book a demo". Each one was somebody's priority. Together
they ask the reader to choose before they know what any of them does.

**Give a page that persuades one primary action, label it with what happens after the click, and repeat it where the reader decides; keep every other action quieter, so nobody has to guess which button matters or what it does.**

> **Interactive demo: Calls To Action.** Open https://critly.vercel.app/calls-to-action to try it.

Start from "Get started" with three solid buttons and squint. Nothing leads.
Drop to one solid button and the page has a point. Then switch the label to
the specific one and read what it promises.

### Name what happens next

In Nielsen Norman Group's testing, a ["Get started"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/get-started/)
button drew clicks from people with almost any goal, then dropped them into a
sign-up flow most of them didn't want yet. Some had come to read about the
company, some to find a detail. The authors' summary: "a link is a promise."
A vague label gets the click and breaks the promise on the next screen.

Kate Moran's [four S's](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/better-link-labels/)
say the same thing about any label: say specifically what's on the other
side, and make sure the next screen delivers it. For a call to action that
usually means naming the thing and, where it matters, the commitment: "Start a
14-day trial", "Book a 20-minute demo", "Try Maker free for 30 days". Put the
reassurance people look for ("No card needed") in a short line next to the
button, not in the label. [Microcopy](https://critly.vercel.app/microcopy) covers verb-first labels in
general.

The label also decides who clicks. "Book a demo" and "Start a trial" suit
different buyers, so pick the one that fits how this product is bought. A
self-serve tool that hides behind a demo form makes the reader wait for a
meeting to see what a trial would have shown them in a minute.

### One action leads

Squint at the first screen (the [taste pass](https://critly.vercel.app/taste-pass) shows how). The
heaviest shape should be the primary action. Repeating that same action in
the nav, after the proof and on the recommended plan doesn't compete with it,
because it's the same promise each time. A different action at the same
weight does compete. Atlassian's rule is [one primary
button](https://atlassian.design/components/button/usage) per area, and it
works for marketing pages too.

Secondary actions still matter. Some readers want the tour, the docs or a
sales call first. Give them an outline button or a text link so they can find
it, and let the primary carry the weight. On a [pricing page](https://critly.vercel.app/pricing-pages)
each plan has its own button, and only the recommended plan's is solid.

### Repeat it where people decide

People don't decide at the top. Some are ready after the headline, more after
the proof, the pricing or the answer to their last objection. Put the same
action at the end of the sections that answer those questions and at the foot
of the page, so the reader never has to scroll back to act.

On a long page, a button that stays on screen can help. Conversion Rate
Experts ran [one test](https://conversion-rate-experts.com/scrolling-tips/) on
a very long sales page where adding a sticky call to action increased sales by
25%. That is one page and one test, so treat it as a reason to try it on long
pages, not a rule for every page. On a phone a sticky bar takes up space the
reader is trying to read in, so keep it to one slim row.

The button needs every [state](https://critly.vercel.app/interaction-states): hover, focus, pressed,
and loading. After the click, show something within [100ms](https://critly.vercel.app/responsiveness). A primary button
that seems to do nothing for a second looks broken at the moment the reader
decided to act.

### Resources

- ["Get Started" stops users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/get-started/): Nielsen Norman Group usability testing on vague "Get started" buttons that lead people into sign-up flows they didn't expect. "A link is a promise."
- [Better link labels](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/better-link-labels/): Kate Moran's four S's for labels that say what's on the other side of the click: specific, sincere, substantial and succinct.
- [Scrolling tips](https://conversion-rate-experts.com/scrolling-tips/): Conversion Rate Experts on long pages, including one test where a sticky call to action raised sales on a long sales page by 25%.
- [Button usage](https://atlassian.design/components/button/usage): Atlassian's rule of one primary button per page or area.


## Pricing Pages

> Real prices, a few plans, the differences.

- Section: Marketing
- URL: https://critly.vercel.app/pricing-pages
- Published: 2026-10-14
- Source: https://github.com/kausthubh-coder/critly/blob/main/content/marketing/pricing-pages.mdx

People open a pricing page with one question: what would this cost me? "Let's
talk" doesn't answer it. Neither does "$29" with no hint of per person or per
month, or five plans with forty rows of checkmarks. Each one hands the reader
the sums, and some of them leave instead.

**Show real prices with what they're per and how often they're billed, offer four plans or fewer with at most one honest recommendation, and compare what differs between plans rather than every feature, because people come to a pricing page to work out what they would pay.**

> **Interactive demo: Pricing Pages.** Open https://critly.vercel.app/pricing-pages to try it.

Flip the billing toggle in both versions. In the confusing one, two prices
change and the rest stay put, and the saving is "up to 20%". In the clear one,
every price changes and each plan states its saving in dollars. Narrow your
window, or open this on a phone, and the confusing table scrolls sideways
while the clear plans stack.

### Show the price

Hiding the price costs more than the extra click. Therese Fessenden at Nielsen
Norman Group lists three [costs](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/b2b-trust-from-b2c/):
it adds a step, it makes the product seem unaffordable, and it "makes the
organization seem dubious for the simple reason that it is hiding something."

If the price really does vary, show what typical customers pay. Jakob Nielsen's
advice from a B2B study is to [show sample prices](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-prices-for-common-scenarios/)
for common orders, for example "$19 per person a month, 25 people minimum"
or "a 40-person team pays about $760 a month." Every price says what it's per
and how often: per person, per month, billed yearly.

### Few plans, one recommendation

Comparing is hard work, and people only do it properly with a few options.
NN/g's guidance on [comparison tables](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/comparison-tables/)
is five items at most, and two at a time on a phone. For plans, three or four
is enough to cover different sizes of customer.

Recommend at most one plan, and say who it's for: "for crews of 2 to 50"
tells a reader more than "Most popular", and it stays true even if it isn't the
plan most people buy. Two badges cancel each other out.

Don't add a plan just to make another look like a bargain. This "decoy"
effect looked strong in early lab work with numbers in a table, but in
Frederick, Lee and Baskin's studies it [largely disappeared](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/The-Limits-of-Attraction.pdf)
when the choices looked like real products, and Yang and Lynn found a
reliable effect in [11 of 91 attempts](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/0c5b506a-f8d3-4a4c-8825-b2c715e9f956).
Every plan on the page should be one that somebody ought to buy.

### Compare the differences

People read a pricing table [row by row](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/07/designing-better-pricing-page/),
left to right, and jump back to the column headers to check which plan they're
looking at. A list of the same eight features under every plan makes them do
that eight times to find the two that differ. Lead each plan with who it's for
and what it adds: "Everything in Solo, plus shared lists and sign-off." If you
need the full table, put it below the plans, keep the plan names in view as
it scrolls, and let people show only the rows that differ.

Set prices in [tabular numbers](https://critly.vercel.app/tabular-numbers), so they line up across
plans and the layout stays still when the toggle changes them.

### The yearly toggle

The toggle changes every price on the page, including the recommended plan,
the per-seat prices and any minimums. State the saving as money for each plan:
"$300 billed yearly, saves $60." "Save up to 20%" makes the reader work out
which plans it applies to. Show the yearly price per month next to the total
billed, so it compares directly with the monthly price, and label the toggle
so it's clear which period is showing.

### On a phone

Plans stack in a single column, and nothing scrolls sideways. Friedman's
advice is to avoid horizontal scrolling in pricing tables "at all costs". A
sideways table hides most of the columns, and the reader loses track of which
plan they're reading. If a detailed comparison has to work on a phone, compare
two plans at a time and let the reader pick which two. See
[responsive](https://critly.vercel.app/responsive) for the rest of the layout.
